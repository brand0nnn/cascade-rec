POSTER §0 · THE EXPERIMENT

First, what counts as a good recommendation?

Given someone’s past reviews, predict the next product they review. A fast retriever makes a shortlist. A ranker chooses the ten products to show.

AMAZON REVIEWS 2023

Cell Phones & Accessories

We use the 5-core review dataset. Reviews are a proxy for purchases—not observed sales or online clicks.

  • Retrieve from all eligible products rather than a sampled set of negative products.
  • Exclude previously reviewed and not-yet-available products.
  • A retrieval miss contributes zero to end-to-end ranking quality.
  • Average per user; use three seeds where stated.
Uncertainty & evaluation limits

Paired 95% intervals use 1,000 user resamples and are conditional on the trained seeds; they are unadjusted. Validation was reused for exploration. Test was evaluated across several studies, not globally untouched. Product availability uses first review as a proxy.

HOW WE EVALUATED · FOLLOW ONE QUERY

From a review history to a measured result.

Each evaluation query asks one specific question: where does the next-reviewed product appear among the recommendations?

Training and model selection differ between study families

The two-tower retriever is trained on period A. Matched-period retrieval baselines use that same period; all-training-data baselines are identified separately. Rankers train on period B.

The MLP/DIN comparison uses saved Validation-selected checkpoints. The residual-ranker study selects an epoch on the last 20% of B, then refits on all of B. Validation explores directions; saved models are fixed before their corresponding Test scoring. These are separate study protocols, not one globally untouched Test.

“Natural-only” training retains queries whose target was retrieved. Sampled non-target candidates have label zero but are unobserved, not confirmed dislikes. The selected recipes sample 50 such negatives per training list. Historical forced-target experiments are separate and are not substituted for the natural-only results shown here.

Methods: docs/experiment_overview.md; docs/research_story.md §1; src/cascade/data/instances.py; src/cascade/eval/harness.py; src/cascade/eval/bootstrap.py

TRY THE METRIC · ILLUSTRATION, NOT EXPERIMENTAL DATA

Finding the target is only half the task.

Move one relevant product through a 20-item example. Recall asks whether it is found; nDCG also rewards a higher position. For one target, nDCG@10 is 1/log₂(rank + 1) inside the top ten, otherwise zero. Chart labels such as 8.57 ×10⁻³ mean 0.00857—not 8.57%.

POSTER §1 · OUR ARCHITECTURE

One pipeline. Different things to learn.

The poster brings together two ranker study families. They share a retrieve-then-rank pipeline, but their features and training recipes differ.

01 Represent the history

The last 50 reviewed products describe the user. The two-tower uses a learned self-attention encoder; recency retrieval uses a time-weighted mean of frozen MiniLM text vectors.

→
02 Retrieve candidates

The two-tower compares learned user and item vectors. Recency compares text similarity to recent history. Mixed lists alternate the two sources, skipping duplicates, until 200 unique products are collected.

→
03 Rank the shortlist

Rank candidate products and display the top ten. The ranker can promote a retrieved target, but cannot add a target absent from the shortlist.

RQ3 · MLP / DIN FAMILY

Which history matters for this candidate?

MLP uses pooled history; DIN adds candidate-conditioned history weighting. Both use retrieval and matching inputs. The comparison includes changed candidate/history interactions, not attention alone.

candidate + history + score → ranker → order

R2–R4 · RESIDUAL FAMILY

How should the retrieval score be corrected?

A BCE-trained MLP learns a correction to a retrieval score. Shared support statistics and optional co-review features describe the candidate and its relationship to the user’s history.

final score = a × z + Δ

z: standardised retrieval score. a: learned scale. Δ: learned correction. The model adjusts an existing retrieval signal.

POSTER §2 · CANDIDATE COUNT

A longer list gives—and takes away.

More candidates recover more targets. They also give a frozen ranker more competitors to choose from. Both effects matter for the final ten.

RETRIEVAL · RECALL@K
FINAL TOP 10 · nDCG@10 × 10⁻³
Why can ranking quality fall when recall rises?

A newly found target can improve the result. But added candidates can also push down targets the original shortlist already ranked well. The saved K-expansion diagnostic separates these two contributions; their sum gives the net change.

POSTER §3 · HIGHER RECALL, SAME RANKER

A better list does not guarantee a better top ten.

Hold the ranker fixed. Expand the candidate count, refresh the retriever, or mix sources. Each changes what reaches the ranker, but not what the ranker has learned.

01

Expand the list

200 → 500 candidates. Recover more targets, while adding competitors beyond the training depth.

02

Refresh the retriever

Train the retriever on a later period. Higher recall can change the mix of candidate products.

03

Mix the sources

Combine recency and two-tower candidates. New membership and scoring change the ranker’s inputs.

What this tells us: higher recall did not consistently improve frozen-ranker performance. Some interventions helped; retraining captured more of the mixed-list gain in the separate R2 control.

POSTER §3 · STATISTICS TIMED AS IN TRAINING

“No reviews yet” changes meaning over time.

The ranker learned from statistics counted at the start of its training period. Reusing that old snapshot years later makes too many products look reviewless.

THE LEARNED CUE

How many products look reviewless?

Target = reviewed next; other = sampled non-target candidates. The training comparison uses the refit’s natural-positive lists.

Consistency is the finding.

Updating to the evaluation period’s start restores the preparation rule used during training. For frozen models, the freshest monthly statistics are not automatically best for new products.

Training and scoring with monthly statistics is a different intervention. With co-review inputs, that retrained system performs well—see R4.

See how co-review features are built

They summarise how often other reviewers connect a candidate to products in the user’s history: shared reviewers, paths and support counts. All counts use reviews before the stated cut date. They are evidence of observed review relationships, not proof that products were purchased together.

Combine the changes.
Understand the comparison.

Mixed lists, monthly training and scoring statistics, and co-review features improve the combined system. The size of the gain depends on the baseline—and the product group.

How clear are the differences?

These four Test comparisons ask different questions. Each row shows its own reference system; they are not four additive steps.

How were these intervals evaluated?

For the same evaluation queries, average each system’s metric across its three trained seeds. Compute differences for each query, then average within each user. Resample users 1,000 times, keeping the compared systems paired. The middle 95% of resampled differences gives the interval. It reflects user sampling conditional on those trained models; it does not capture every source of uncertainty.

The best aggregate system is not the best for every product group. Switch to New products: recency lists with monthly co-review features beat the mixed-list system in this study.

POSTER §§4–6 · ANALYSIS, CONCLUSION & LIMITATIONS

What the shadows taught us.

01

Inspect retrieval first.

The ranker cannot recover an absent target. Candidate recall sets a ceiling on end-to-end success.

02

Check adaptation.

Better candidates do not guarantee better ranking. Training on the new inputs captured more of the gain here.

03

Match the timing rules.

Prepare features consistently in training and use. “Fresher” alone does not describe the experiment.

What these experiments cannot establish
  • Offline next-review prediction does not measure online user satisfaction or sales.
  • Crawl-time titles can contain later information; the all-time 5-core filter can bias new-product findings.
  • Validation reuse and repeated Test exposure limit generalisation; intervals are exploratory and unadjusted.
  • A combined pipeline comparison cannot identify the contribution of every individual change.
  • New products are defined by first review in the evaluation period, not verified market launch.
Appendix · What about HNSW and IVF?

In this fixed 1,000-query benchmark, adaptive HNSW preserved measured top-10 quality. IVF traded quality for speed. Retaining most exact candidates does not imply retaining all relevant purchases.
